# Live Conflict Protocol

When a stakeholder pushes back in the room:

## Acknowledge

- Validate the concern without conceding your position.
- Name the specific concern.
- Maintain a neutral tone.
- Avoid premature concessions.

Example:

   > "That's a fair concern. Operational continuity is exactly what we need to protect."

## Reframe

- Redirect from opinion to business outcomes.
- Restate in impact terms.
- Link to strategic objectives.
- Cite data or precedent.

Example:

   > "When we look at our downtime cost per incident, averaging $50K, the phased rollback approach actually reduces exposure rather than adding it."

## Anchor

- Tie back to the agreed decision criteria.
- Show how the recommendation outperforms alternatives on the dimensions the room already cares about.
- Close with the decision request.

Example:

   > "Given our shared goal of maintaining 99.9% uptime while reducing OpEx, this approach is the one that satisfies both constraints. Do we have alignment to proceed?"

## Post-Meeting Follow-Up

Send a written decision record to all attendees within 24 hours. This creates
accountability and prevents "I don't remember agreeing to that" a week later.

Structure the record around the outcome:

**If approved:**
> "Thank you for the time today. We have alignment to proceed with [recommendation].
> Owner: [name]. First milestone: [deliverable] by [date]. I'll send a status update
> on [date]."

**If deferred:**
> "Thank you for the discussion. The decision is on hold pending [specific condition].
> Once [condition] is resolved, I'll request a 30-minute follow-up. I'll have [what]
> ready by [date]."

Do not accept an open-ended deferral. If the meeting ends without a clear condition for
reconvening, send a follow-up that names one: "To move forward, I understand we need
[specific thing]. Is that correct?" Get confirmation in writing.

**If denied:**
> "Thank you for the clarity. I heard the decision is no because [reason]. I'll revisit
> this if [condition changes]. Please let me know if I've misunderstood the concern."

A denial with a named reason is useful — it tells you exactly what to resolve before
the next attempt.
